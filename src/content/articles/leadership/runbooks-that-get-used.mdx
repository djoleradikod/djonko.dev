<InShort>
A runbook is a **how-to**, not an explanation. It starts with **when to use it** and what you need before starting. Each step is one action with a **check** that proves it worked. It says **when to stop** and who to call. It links to background rather than containing it. And it has an **owner** and a **last-used date**. VCF 9 moved a lot of UI paths, so every 5.x runbook needs a review against the new consoles before the upgrade, not after.
</InShort>

## A template

```text
Title:     <verb> <object>  (e.g. "Remediate a disconnected ESX root account")
Use when:  <symptom or trigger>
Do not use when: <cases that need something else>
Needs:     <access, credentials, tools, approvals>
Time:      <measured duration>

1. <action>
   Check: <what you should see>
2. <action>
   Check: <what you should see>
...
Stop and escalate if: <condition>  ->  <who, how>
Background: <links to the explanation, KBs>
Owner: <name/role>   Last used: <date>   Last reviewed: <date>
```

## Reviewing runbooks for VCF 9

- **Paths**: anything that says "SDDC Manager UI" may now be VCF Operations or vCenter. See [where did that button go](/vcf/library/day-2/where-did-that-button-go/).
- **Credentials**: system-managed service accounts change who you log in as.
- **New components**: the License Server and the services runtime need their own runbooks, at least for power events and health checks.

<Callout type="field">
After every use, the person who ran the runbook fixes one thing in it, even a typo. The people who use a runbook see what has gone stale, so have them maintain it instead of a separate documentation effort.
</Callout>

## Pitfalls

- **Screenshots as steps.** They age with every release. Describe the path in words.
- **"Restart services if needed."** Say which, when, and how to tell.
- **No stop rule.** People keep going because the document doesn't say not to.
