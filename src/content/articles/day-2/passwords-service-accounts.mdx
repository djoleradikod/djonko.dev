<InShort>
VCF has three kinds of accounts. **Local** accounts are OS logins such as root. **Application** accounts are product admins. **Service** accounts are system-generated credentials one component uses to call another. In 5.x, SDDC Manager generated and rotated them, the guidance was to rotate every 80 days, and from 5.2.1 you could also manage them from the vSphere Client. In 9.x most inter-component service accounts **rotate automatically every 30 days** by default, through VCF Operations or SDDC Manager. TechDocs is explicit that external PAM tools must not manage them. 9.0 is a transition: rotation for several management components is not yet automated. In 9.1.1 even SDDC Manager's own local passwords move into VCF Operations.
</InShort>

## Three loops to recognise

**1. Out-of-band change.** Someone changes a root or service account password directly on the component. VCF's vault still holds the old value, the account shows as Disconnected, and the next lifecycle task fails. Fix it by remediating with the current working password (<KB id={436398} />). In 9.1, if an SDDC lifecycle service account was rotated or deleted out of band, the runtime's stored secret is stale. Upgrades fail with `InvalidLogin`, and repeated attempts get the runtime addresses blocked by SDDC Manager. The fix scripts and service restarts are in <KB id={450375} />.

**2. Lockout loop.** A service keeps logging in to NSX with a stale credential, the account locks, rotation fails, and the service locks it again. Break the loop by temporarily adding SDDC Manager to NSX's lockout-immune addresses, rotating, verifying, then removing the exception (<KB id={420736} />, <KB id={449684} />).

**3. Expired admin.** SDDC Manager uses the NSX `admin` account for the API. When it expires, NSX managers show as disconnected and rotation of admin and audit fails. Reset it on the NSX node, then Remediate (<KB id={438020} />).

## Locks: the side effect that blocks everything else

A failed password task can hold a lock on a domain. That lock then blocks backups, host commissioning or an upgrade switchover hours later. The supported first step is to cancel the credential task through the API (<KB id={444986} />). Database lock removal is a last resort, after a snapshot, and only under the KB. A stale lock combined with an out-of-band root change on ESX has its own combined procedure (<KB id={449686} />).

```bash
# SDDC Manager: find failed credential tasks, then cancel the one holding the lock
curl -sk -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" https://localhost/v1/credentials/tasks | jq '.elements[] | {id,status,name}'
curl -sk -X DELETE -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" https://localhost/v1/credentials/tasks/<task-id>
```

## In 9.1: a missing service account

If a remediation recreated the SDDC Manager to vCenter service account as an ordinary SSO user, the upgrade precheck reports that the service account was not found. The fix recreates it as a real service account with the stored secret and the required groups (<KB id={449131} />).

<Callout type="warn" title="PAM tools and VCF">
A security team may ask to bring every privileged account into its PAM tool. For VCF service accounts the answer is no, and TechDocs says so. Offer the alternative: human admin accounts go through PAM and SSO. Service accounts are rotated by VCF, and VCF Operations alerts on their state.
</Callout>

## Pitfalls

- **Rotating everything at once before an upgrade.** A failed rotation leaves a lock behind. Rotate in a separate change, and confirm no failed tasks remain.
- **Long passwords pasted into UIs with limits.** One known remediation failure is a 127-character UI limit.
- **Password expiry settings on ESX changed by hand.** Changing ESX expiry days outside VCF has disconnected hosts in Password Management. Keep policy changes inside VCF.
