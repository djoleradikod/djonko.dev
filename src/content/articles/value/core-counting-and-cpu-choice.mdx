<InShort>
Choose the product by its floor, then choose the CPU by running the sum below at your real capacity target. No CPU size wins everywhere. At 120 cores with N+1, dual 16-core and dual 20-core hosts tie at 160 licensed cores, and the 16-core build is the safer buy because the 20-core build jumps to 200 at 121 cores. On VCF or VVF, a CPU under 16 cores never licenses fewer cores than a 16-core part. The 72-core order minimum isn't in Broadcom's program documents, counting KB or Licensing Model (<AsOf date="2026-11-06" />), so treat it as a reseller term to get in writing. This isn't legal or purchasing advice: your order and the current program documentation decide.
</InShort>

<Callout type="note" title="Corrected on 6 November 2026">
This note said dual 20-core needed the fewest licensed cores and the fewest hosts, and that a 100-core target changed the answer; in fact dual 16-core ties it at 160, dual 32-core uses fewer hosts, and 100 gives the same winners as 120. It also omitted the Edge site rules and dated the first 72-core report to April 2025, when The Register ran it on 28 March.
</Callout>

## The rules, by product

| | VCF | VVF | VCF Edge | vSphere Express |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Floor per CPU | 16 cores | 16 cores | 8 cores | Not stated for Express |
| Disabled cores | Licensed | Licensed | Licensed | Not stated for Express |
| Per order or estate | None published | None published | At least 10 Edge Locations within a year of first deployment | At most 128 cores per customer |
| Per site | None published | None published | At most 256 cores | Console blocks allocation above 128 "per customer or site ID"; default licence can't be shared across sites |
| vSAN per core | 1 TiB | 0.25 TiB, rounded up in KB 313548 | 1 TiB, usable only across Edge cores | No add-ons; the page doesn't mention vSAN |

Count sockets and cores and leave logical processors out. Every core on the server needs a licence, "including Cores deactivated by the BIOS", and a core is "a single physical computational unit", in all three June 2026 program documents ([VCF](https://ftpdocs.broadcom.com/cadocs/0/VCF_SPD_June2026.pdf), [VVF](https://ftpdocs.broadcom.com/cadocs/0/VVF_SPD_June2026.pdf), [Edge](https://ftpdocs.broadcom.com/cadocs/0/VCF_Edge_SPD_June2026.pdf)). The VCF, VVF and Edge columns come from those documents, with VVF's rounding from <KB id={313548} />, and [FAQ](https://www.vmware.com/docs/vmware-cloud-foundation-9-1-general-faqs) Q75 (4 Nov 2026) repeats the Edge figures.

Express rests on thinner evidence. Its column is the [TechDocs page](https://techdocs.broadcom.com/us/en/vmware-cis/vsphere/vsphere/9-1/vsphere-express.html) of 2 Nov 2026, and no Express program document could be found on 6 Nov 2026. [Licensing Model](https://techdocs.broadcom.com/us/en/vmware-cis/vcf/vcf-9-0-and-later/9-1/licensing/licensing-overview/licensing-model.html) (5 Nov 2026) lists Express as a core-based licence and applies the 16-core floor to "most products" without saying whether Express is one, so the Express example further down treats the floor as an assumption.

## Which CPU licenses the fewest cores

The sum for a dual-socket host:

**licensed cores = (target ÷ physical cores per host, rounded up, + spare hosts) × 2 × the larger of cores per CPU and 16**

This example is constructed. The target is the physical cores the workload needs with the spare hosts out of service, there is one spare host (N+1), and every CPU is assumed to do equal work per core. That last assumption is false in practice, so replace the target with per-core benchmark figures for the actual models before a real purchase.

| Dual-socket CPU | Licensed per host | 96 target | 120 target | 144 target | 200 target |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12-core (24 physical) | 32 | 5 hosts, 160 | 6 hosts, 192 | 7 hosts, 224 | 10 hosts, 320 |
| 16-core (32 physical) | 32 | 4 hosts, **128** | 5 hosts, **160** | 6 hosts, **192** | 8 hosts, 256 |
| 20-core (40 physical) | 40 | 4 hosts, 160 | 4 hosts, **160** | 5 hosts, 200 | 6 hosts, **240** |
| 24-core (48 physical) | 48 | 3 hosts, 144 | 4 hosts, 192 | 4 hosts, **192** | 6 hosts, 288 |
| 32-core (64 physical) | 64 | 3 hosts, 192 | 3 hosts, 192 | 4 hosts, 256 | 5 hosts, 320 |

Checking one cell: for 20-core at 120, 120 ÷ 40 = 3 hosts, plus 1 spare = 4 hosts, and 4 × 40 = 160.

Bold marks the lowest count in each column. Dual 16-core wins at 96, ties at 120 and 144, and loses to dual 20-core at 200, so run the sum at your own target instead of borrowing a winner from this table.

The 12-core row never wins: its hosts license 32 cores like the 16-core hosts but deliver 24, so at every target it needs at least as many hosts, and at 120 it pays for 6 × 8 = 48 cores that don't exist. Dual 32-core has the fewest hosts at 120 and still loses, with 3 hosts licensing 192, and each spare host costs 64.

The tie at 120 breaks on headroom. After a host failure, the 16-core build has 4 × 32 = 128 cores against the 120 target, and the 20-core build has 3 × 40 = 120, with nothing left over. One core of growth shows the difference: at 121 cores the 16-core build still licenses 160, and the 20-core build needs a fifth host and licenses 200.

<Callout type="analysis" title="Breaking the tie at 120">
Buy dual 16-core at a 120 target unless the workload is truly capped, in which case dual 20-core saves a fifth server at the same licence count. A multiple of 40 doesn't settle it: dual 20-core wins at 200 and 240, ties at 280, and loses at 160 and 320. Run the sum at today's target and at your three-year target, and pick the build that stays lowest across both.
</Callout>

Four things can overrule the core count:

- A design that survives a host in maintenance and a failure together (N+2) changes the 120 column to 12-core 224, 16-core 192, 20-core 200, 24-core 240 and 32-core 256, and dual 16-core then wins alone.
- If memory runs out before CPU, memory per host sets the host count, whatever the core sum says. [NVMe memory tiering](/vcf/library/value/nvme-memory-tiering/) changes that limit.
- The lower count also brings less vSAN: on VCF, 160 licensed cores bring 160 TiB and 192 bring 192 TiB. Check the [entitlement note](/vcf/library/value/vsan-entitlement/) before you take it.
- On an upgraded cluster, vSAN protection can move the answer. In 9.1, Auto-RAID uses RAID-5 2+1 on three to five hosts and RAID-6 4+2 from six, both at 1.5 times raw, according to the [space efficiency guide](https://www.vmware.com/docs/vmw-vsan-space-efficiency). Before 9.1, six or more hosts could use 4+1 at 1.25 times, so a build that crosses six hosts on an upgraded cluster needs more raw capacity for the same data. More on this in [vSAN ESA space efficiency](/vcf/library/value/vsan-esa-space-efficiency/).

## When VCF Edge changes the count

The lower floor only saves licences on CPUs under 16 cores. This example is constructed: 12 retail sites, each with two single-CPU 8-core hosts. Edge needs 12 × 2 × 8 = 192 cores. Standard VCF needs 12 × 2 × 16 = 384. With 16-core CPUs at the same sites, both count 384, and the choice comes down to your quote and the Edge terms.

Three limits come with Edge, all from the June 2026 [Edge program document](https://ftpdocs.broadcom.com/cadocs/0/VCF_Edge_SPD_June2026.pdf). They set where it can run, how many sites it takes and how large a site can be:

- An Edge Location is a remote site such as a branch store, fulfilment centre or substation. Data centres, colocation and public cloud are excluded, even for internal use.
- You may not have deployed fewer than 10 Edge Locations within the first year, the document says. With one 8-core CPU per site that is at least 10 × 8 = 80 cores.
- No site may exceed 256 cores. Dual 32-core hosts reach that at four hosts (4 × 64), so a fifth at 320 is over.

The entitlement stays apart too. Edge vSAN TiB can only be used across cores running Edge vSphere, so it can't cover a storage-heavy cluster in the main data centre.

Recount any CPU of 8 to 15 cores at an Edge site by hand at the 8-core floor. The counting tool is described as applying "a minimum of 16 cores per physical CPU", with no mention of Edge (<KB id={313548} />), so expect it to overstate those CPUs. That's an inference from the KB's wording.

## What 128 cores buys on Express

The 128-core cap covers the whole customer, so Express has to fit the estate as well as the cluster. It is "for customers with 128 cores across all environments" in Broadcom's [4 Nov post](https://blogs.vmware.com/cloud-foundation/2026/11/04/broadcom-vcf-portfolio-updates/) and "A maximum of 128 cores per customer" on [TechDocs](https://techdocs.broadcom.com/us/en/vmware-cis/vsphere/vsphere/9-1/vsphere-express.html). Price, term, pack size and who qualifies are reseller reports, weighed in the [portfolio note](/vcf/library/licensing/november-2026-portfolio/) with each party's stake named.

This example is constructed and uses that note's assumptions: the 16-core floor applies and every host counts. It adds the column a design needs, which is what's left after one host fails.

| Host | Licensed per host | Hosts within 128 | Licensed total | Physical cores left after one failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 × 16-core | 32 | 4 | 128 | 96 |
| 2 × 24-core | 48 | 2 | 96 | 48 |
| 2 × 32-core | 64 | 2 | 128 | 64 |
| 1 × 32-core | 32 | 4 | 128 | 96 |
| 1 × 16-core | 16 | 8 | 128 | 112 |

If the workload needs more than 112 cores with a host down, Express can't hold it with these CPUs, which leaves VVF or VCF. Under the floor assumption, smaller sockets and more hosts leave the most after a failure. A dual 24-core design strands 32 of the 128, because a third host would reach 144.

## The 72-core order minimum

Size to the per-CPU floor and let 72 change the number only when a reseller writes it into your quote, naming the clause. It only matters on small orders. Three single-CPU hosts at the floor come to 3 × 16 = 48 cores, so a 72-core minimum would add 24 unused. An Edge estate that meets the 10-site rule reaches at least 80, though phased orders could sit under 72.

The June 2026 program documents, <KB id={313548} /> and Licensing Model carry the per-CPU floors and no order minimum (checked 6 Nov 2026), and no Broadcom document cited here states a 72-core minimum. The reports that name a source for 72 point to a distributor email or a reseller pricebook. A price list term wouldn't necessarily appear in those documents, and no primary source settles what resellers can sell today, which is why the clause has to be in writing.

The two latest entries below conflict, and both come from firms with something to sell, so neither settles it.

## Before you sign

Run the counting tool from <KB id={313548} /> against the real hosts with all cores enabled, since disabled cores can make its output inaccurate, the KB warns. On vSAN 8.0 U3 use the updated script in <KB id={400416} />. It needs PowerCLI 13.3 or later and PowerShell 7.4.6 or later. Look for CPUs under the floor in hosts bought at different times: each of those is a host where a 16-core replacement gives more capacity for the same licence.
