## Where the summary and the source part ways

**Pilot or production.** Shenoy's post says the study reflects enterprises "running VCF 9 in production". Forrester's method section says it surveyed people "at organizations using VCF 9 in a pilot or beta stage". Use Forrester's wording. The study's title says "Projected", and the post's headline says "Achieved".

**Independent or commissioned.** The post says "These aren't vendor claims." Forrester's disclosures say the study "is commissioned by Broadcom", that Broadcom reviewed it and supplied the interview names, and that Forrester kept editorial control while a third party fielded a double-blind survey. Write all of that in your footnote. Forrester also says it "makes no assumptions as to the potential ROI that other organizations will receive", and that the study "is not meant to be used as a competitive analysis", so it can't carry a comparison against another platform.

**Modelled peak or surveyed figure.** The blog-ready numbers are high-case inputs: 72% tickets against 60% surveyed, 50% delivery against 18%, 32% storage downtime against 26%, 15% utilisation against 10%. The surveyed figure is the safer one to quote.

**TCO or not.** The Signal65 report says it describes "a combined five-year cost rather than a total cost of ownership", because facilities, migration, training, downtime and decommissioning are outside it. Broadcom's post calls it storage TCO.

**Same digits, different claims.** At Explore, 39% was overall server cost, per Fenton. In Romero's post it is storage TCO against arrays. The 42% is "hardware expenses" in one and "server TCO" in the other. Check which one your slide means before it travels.

## Using one of these in a business case

<Callout type="analysis" title="This site's analysis, and not purchasing advice">
These steps are reasoning from the sources above. They don't show any study to be wrong, and a figure that fails them may still be a fair description of its own scenario. Pricing and contract questions belong with your reseller and Broadcom's current terms.
</Callout>

**Quote the whole claim.** A line that survives review reads: "Forrester Consulting, commissioned by Broadcom (August 2026), projects 31% to 137% three-year ROI for a composite $3 billion enterprise, built from users in a pilot or beta stage, with no net-new hardware modelled." Keep those qualifiers on the slide with the figure.

**Sort benefits by whether a bill changes.** Shenoy gives the high-case shares: 37% infrastructure, 34% operations effort, 12% downtime, 10% delivery, 4% retired tools, 3% security. Forrester describes the infrastructure line as avoided or deferred spend, which lands at a refresh or not at all, and the operations line as redeployed time. Of the six, retired tools is the line closest to an invoice that stops. [Building the cost case](/vcf/library/value/building-the-cost-case/) makes the same point: show savings in the year they land.

**Re-run it without the softest line.** This is a constructed test using Forrester's published totals, with costs held at $15,229,237 (benefits minus NPV in either end case). Remove the infrastructure line at its own PV in each scenario, $6,043,050, $9,027,273 and $13,429,001 (the mid value is $3.63M a year discounted at 10%):

| Scenario | Benefits PV as published | Benefits PV without that line | ROI as published | ROI without it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | $20,018,185 | $13,975,135 | 31% | −8% |
| Mid | $26,393,152 | $17,365,879 | 73% | 14% |
| High | $36,069,048 | $22,640,047 | 137% | 49% |

That line rests on the $60M annual spend, an interview-based input. Put your own spend through the same formula, and your own quote in place of the $5.76M annual subscription, which is 94% of the cost PV. Forrester "strongly advises" readers to use their own estimates, and the middle case is a fairer opener than the headline. Then present the pessimistic result first, as the cost case note sets out.

**Match the starting point.** The TEI starts from fragmented tools and a partly virtualised estate, so a shop already standardised on VCF has less of that to gain. Its interviewees run from 700 employees to 200,000, and the Spotlight's 70% comes from the insurer with 700. Signal65's 39.5% needs 64 hosts and 2,148 TB on list prices; at 12 hosts it is 13.5%. Get a dated quote for the array you'd really buy, because the report also applies no discount on either side.

**Don't stack percentages.** A 42% tiering figure and a 34% storage figure come from separate estimates on overlapping hardware budgets. Apply levers in the fixed order the cost case note gives, recomputing hosts each time, and let [the tiering decision tree](/vcf/library/value/nvme-memory-tiering/) decide whether tiering applies at all. Its worked example shows the saving vanishing above about 51% active memory. Read the 40 to 45% tiering figures as ceilings, since each is stated as "up to".

**Date the price inputs.** WWT used about $40 per GB of DRAM and $1 per GB of NVMe in early 2026. Principled Technologies' Dell.com prices of 8 June 2026 work out at about $103.59 per GB ($106,077.28 for 1,024 GB) and $2.81 per GB ($10,783.48 for a 3.84 TB drive), by this site's arithmetic. Those are different price bases as well as different dates. The ratio barely moves, near 40:1 and 37:1, while the DRAM price per GB differs by more than two and a half times, so reprice any memory case on the day it's submitted. Romero cites DRAM rises of "80 to 170% year over year in 2026".

Percentages also say nothing about what tiering does to the workload. For that, Epping's [Unexplored Territory episode #103](https://www.yellow-bricks.com/2025/09/22/103-the-performance-impact-of-memory-tiering-featuring-qasim-ali-and-todd-muirhead/) (22 September 2025) with Qasim Ali and Todd Muirhead covers performance impact and links the white paper; it has no cost figures, so use it beside this ledger.
